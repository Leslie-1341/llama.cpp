The last cookie for me presents an opportunity to reflect on fairness. When a new box of cookies is opened in a group of friends and family, we want everyone to have equal and fair access to the cookies. We are a family of four brothers, so we have to make sure everyone gets their fair share if we want to avoid fighting and instead enjoy our cookies in peace. While the box is still full, two things happen: firstly, it seems there are plenty of cookies for everyone. Secondly, exactly keeping track of who eats how many cookies is simply too much effort, so noone bothers. Once the box becomes emptier, however, we start to become more aware of the scarcity of the cookies we have. In German, there is a beautiful word for this: "wegessen", literally "to eat something away", meaning to "eat something with the consequence that others don't have it anymore". We try not to "eat the cookies away" from others, to avoid brotherly fights. The last cookie then is the epitome of cookie scarcity: it is the last chance to ensure fairness. Whoever had the least cookies so far should get the last one. This is not always easy to evaluate, so often the last cookie stays in the box much longer than any other cookie. Before taking it, one must make sure that all the others already had their fair share. Unless, you know, you're up for a fight.

I frequently observe the same phenomenon with other small consumption goods. Recently, this New Years Eve, I bought firecrackers for my two nephews and my niece. There were plenty, so each of them took as many as they liked, and had their fun. They were harmless, I did not have to supervise them much, neither in taking them out of the box nor in lighting them up. They were not concerned with how many the others took, as there were clearly enough for everybody. However, once they got down to a small number of firecrackers, maybe ten or so, each one of the kids started to become weary of the others. Did they take too many? Did they rob me of the chance of having my fair share? In the face of scarcity, they started to quarrel, and I had to remind them to be nice to each other. Once I had done that, and made clear I would not tolerate any fighting, they started to take turns until there was only one firecracker left. The last firecracker changed the situation again: it brought to mind the consideration of fairness, as it was the last chance to make sure that everybody got an equal share. After again some quarrels, and my reminder not to fight, they agreed to break the pattern of the turns they took before, and give the last firecracker to my niece. They did this as she claimed to have taken the least number of firecrackers so far.

I might of course have inadvertantly influenced my niece and nephews to play fair in the way I wanted them to. The situation serves as an example of my own perception of the 'last cookie phenomenon' - as a phenomenon of scarcity, and an opportunity to reflect on fairness.
